Low-Latency Fault Detection for Power Inverters

FPGA-based open-switch diagnosis in electric drives

Published at CAE 2026 — G.E.A., UNRC

← Back to portfolio
Published · CAE 2026

Overview

An FPGA-based strategy for detecting and isolating open-circuit faults in the power switches of electric-drive inverters. The method exploits the characteristic behavior of phase currents in the zero-crossing region, combining lightweight EMA filtering, a hysteresis band, a Newton-Raphson time estimator, and derivative-sign analysis. Detection latency is approximately 10% of the electrical period, using about 6% of LUTs and 5% of flip-flops on an Artix-7 XC7A35T at 100 MHz.

Problem

Open-circuit faults in inverter switches do not stop the drive abruptly like short-circuits do. Instead, they subtly distort phase currents, cause unbalanced conduction, induce torque ripple, and create long-term thermal and electrical stress on the remaining components — which can lead to secondary failures. Fast, reliable diagnosis is essential for industrial drives, traction systems, and high-dependability applications.

Solution

A model-free, current-based method implemented entirely in FPGA using fixed-point arithmetic. The algorithm uses only measured phase currents — no additional sensors, no coordinate transformations, no observers. It combines:

  • An exponential moving average (EMA) filter for noise reduction — α chosen as a power of two (0.5 or 0.25) so it maps to shifts
  • A hysteresis band around zero (±15% of dynamic range) to define the zero-crossing region robustly against noise
  • A time estimator based on Newton-Raphson linear approximation: s[k] = −y[k] / ẏ[k], where ẏ[k] is the backward Euler derivative
  • A zero-crossing integrator that counts the actual samples inside the hysteresis band
  • A comparator: fault is reported when the integrator exceeds the estimated time
  • Fault isolation using the signs of the current derivatives to distinguish upper vs. lower switch faults
  • A simultaneous zero-crossing detector that disables diagnosis during cross-switch transients

Architecture

  • Target: Artix-7 XC7A35T (Digilent Basys 3)
  • Language: Verilog HDL, simulated in Vivado
  • Fixed-point format: Q12 (0 integer bits, 12 fractional bits), two's complement
  • ADC: dual-channel 12-bit, 1 MSPS; sampled in parallel at 3 kS/s; phase c reconstructed as i_c = −i_a − i_b
  • Divider: Vivado IP, radix-2, signed two's complement
  • Base clock: 100 MHz
  • Resource usage: ~6% of LUTs, ~5% of flip-flops
  • Detection latency: ≈ 10% of the electrical period (≈ 0.1 T)
  • Test bench: 0.75 kW induction motor, 48 V DC bus, controlled by a TMS320F28335 DSP running field-oriented control (FOC) and space-vector PWM at 10 kHz
  • Current transducers: LEM LA 50-S/SP1 closed-loop Hall-effect
  • Fault injection: deactivating switching signals from the DSP
  • Logic analyzer: Saleae Logic 8 for latency measurement

My role

R&D and FPGA implementation: algorithm study, RTL design in Verilog, pipelining and optimization, fixed-point design (Q12), timing closure in Vivado, integration with the power electronics hardware, and full experimental validation — MATLAB simulation of each module, Proteus, hardware-in-the-loop on the Artix-7, and bench testing on a real induction motor drive. Co-author of the resulting publication.

Results

  • Detection latency: ≈ 10% of the electrical period (≈ 0.1 T), an order of magnitude faster than most phase-current-based methods reported in the literature (typically ≤ 1 T)
  • FPGA resource usage: ~6% LUTs, ~5% flip-flops on Artix-7 XC7A35T
  • Validated in simulation (MATLAB, Proteus), hardware-in-the-loop, and bench testing on a 0.75 kW induction motor drive
  • Correct isolation between upper and lower switch faults, including single-switch, full-leg, and cross-switch scenarios
  • Published in Proc. 8th Argentine Conference on Electronics (CAE), UNT, 2026
